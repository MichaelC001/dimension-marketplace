# The Story Strip — a minimum viable empathy story

A one-page, six-panel comic strip of the customer's slice of life. It's the
cheapest way to build real empathy: screens tell you **WHAT** happens; a story
tells you **WHY** and **HOW**. Screens pull you toward your product (a sunk-cost
trap); the Story Strip keeps you on the customer's problem.

Output is six panels, each with **≤ 5 words** of caption and a rough sketch
(stick figures are fine — see Pareidolia below). It is a *slice of the
customer's life*, not a click-path through your UI.

---

## Construction order (do NOT go left-to-right)

1. **Panel 6 first — the happy ending, ≤ 5 words.** Decide where the customer
   ends up *feeling good*. Anchoring the resolution first keeps every earlier
   panel pointed at a real outcome, not at your feature list.
2. **Panels 1–5 next — ≤ 5 words each.** Panel 1 = the customer's ordinary
   context / trigger. Panels 2–5 = the middle.
3. **Put the conflict in panels 2–5.** No struggle = no story = no empathy.
   The customer must hit friction, doubt, or a wall before panel 6 resolves it.
4. **Illustrate each panel.** Focus on the customer's *emotions, context, and
   thoughts* — what they feel, not what your buttons do.

> **Rule of thumb:** if any panel names your product's UI ("taps Sign Up"),
> it's probably wrong. Rewrite it as what the *customer* is experiencing
> ("stuck, running out of time").

---

## Improvement rules

Run a drafted strip through these to make it carry empathy:

- **Start with empathy** — the customer's problem, not app steps.
- **Capture emotions** — convey a mini roller-coaster of ups and downs, not a flat line.
- **Focus on actions** — make the reader *feel* what the protagonist goes through.
- **Highlight the hero's struggles** — concentrate the conflict in panels 2–5.
- **Look for improvement opportunities** — mark the gap *between two panels* where
  something could go wrong or be made better. That gap is your design target
  (hand it to the `journey-map` skill as a Pit, or the `psych-audit` skill as a
  friction point).

### Feeding research back in

After empathy research (see [empathy-questions.md](empathy-questions.md)), update
the strip with real **hopes** (→ shape panel 6 and the mood of panel 1),
**pains** (→ the conflict in 2–5), and **barriers** (→ the specific moment the
hero gets stuck). A Barrier-question story maps almost 1:1 onto panels 2–5.

## The psychology (why 6 panels, why comics) {#psychology}

**Storytelling:**
- **Narrative Bias** — brains make sense of the world through stories, so a
  story sticks where a spec slides off.
- **Singularity Effect** — you empathize far more with **one** named person than
  with "users" or a segment. Write for one hero.
- **Character Identification** — following a hero's journey makes the reader's
  brain feel like it's living it, manufacturing empathy.

**Comics:**
- **Closure** — readers fill the gaps *between* panels; that gap-filling is a
  forcing function for creativity (yours and theirs).
- **Miller's Law** — working memory holds 7 ± 2 items, so **6** panels are
  graspable at a glance. More panels → the map stops being usable.
- **Pareidolia** — humans read faces and emotion into abstract shapes, so even
  stick figures build empathy. Don't wait for a designer; sketch it now.

---

## Common mistakes

- **Product-path, not life-slice.** Six screenshots of your funnel is not a
  Story Strip. Zoom out to the customer's day.
- **No conflict.** A smooth happy path teaches nothing — panels 2–5 must hurt.
- **Ending first? skipped.** Writing panel 1→6 in order tends to drift into
  feature narration. Lock panel 6 first.
- **Captions too long.** > 5 words per panel = you're explaining, not showing.
- **A committee of heroes.** "Users" dilutes the Singularity Effect — one person.
